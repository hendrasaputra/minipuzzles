# Pixel effects and physics plan

**Review proposal · 4 November 2026**

Use short pixel effects to show what a move did, where it happened, and when it finished. Keep the rotatable 3D boards, readable clues, and the distinction between the goal and the current board. Start with **Petals**, **Water Balance**, and **Rolling Stone**. Together they test an impact, moving water, and the weight of a solid object.

## What PixelFX offers

[PixelFX](https://github.com/patch-gg/pixelfx/tree/a84159688af3ca37a4be05a0d422b94451b2e24e) provides procedural 2D pixel effects. The reviewed revision lists 244 effects across 21 categories. Effects can be recolored, mirrored, and seeded. Some draw directly from time, while others simulate particles at a fixed 120 Hz. Its exporter produces transparent sprite sheets with frame timings and pivot metadata. The character animation system demonstrates anticipation, contact, and recovery. That timing is useful for our pieces and robots even when the source character art does not fit. [README](https://github.com/patch-gg/pixelfx/blob/a84159688af3ca37a4be05a0d422b94451b2e24e/README.md) · [Effect catalog](https://github.com/patch-gg/pixelfx/blob/a84159688af3ca37a4be05a0d422b94451b2e24e/docs/CATALOG.md)

**Suggested integration.** Export only a few effects at the pinned revision, shorten and recolor them, and play them as nearest-filtered textures on small Three.js planes. Place floor effects flat on the board and upright effects at the relevant world position. Use depth testing so blocks and other floors can hide effects appropriately. Keep effects out of picking and leave numbers, controls, and guides crisp. Check the effect-only export for unwanted characters, lettering, or baked backgrounds before choosing it. Use existing Three.js geometry for directional water, beams, and long paths that must follow the scene. Consider live PixelFX simulation only if the sprite and native 3D versions cannot produce the cue we need.

PixelFX code and effects use MIT. Retain the license and applicable upstream notices for copied code or exported effects. Exclude the gallery font, which has separate terms. ## Physics and readability

- **The rules decide the move.** Existing engines determine legal positions, clues, connections, quantities, and wins. Render a committed move with acceleration, a pivot, or a short settle. Land exactly on its recorded position. A rejected move gets a brief cue at the obstruction. Undo, Retry, and level changes cancel stale effects and show the resulting board.
- **Use only the physics each cue needs.** For a landing or pickup, a damped spring or a ballistic arc is enough. Loose particles can fall, collide with the local floor, bounce once, then fade. Water uses a tilted surface and a short damped ripple. Ice and rolling objects ease into their prescribed stops. Keep any game-changing experiments, such as friction or ramps in Marble Workshop, in a separate prototype with new levels and explicit rules.
- **Run effects with the existing scene clock.** MiniScene games share MaterialMotion, including its 30 fps update cap, visibility handling, reduced motion, and cached-shadow repaint. Petals has its own scene and needs its own hook into the same playback conventions. Use one bounded effect pool per active board, seed it independently from puzzle generation, clamp elapsed time on resume, and dispose it on scene replacement. Use a small fixed simulation step only for live particles. Avoid introducing a second renderer or a general physics engine for these cues.
- **Make contact readable.** A small wind-up, visible contact, and short settle are usually enough. Keep effects local and avoid camera shake, screen flashes, input lock, and full-board covers. Reduced motion uses a still outline or status cue. Every important result also remains visible through shape, text, or the board state.

## Suggested order of work

1. **Make three small prototypes.** Petals gets the single mine impact, Water Balance gets the receiving splash and settle, and Rolling Stone gets the stance-specific contact. Compare each against its current live behavior. Show an effect-only and an integrated capture for each.
2. **Review before rolling out.** Choose the smallest reusable cues that make cause and effect clearer. Verify 320 px portrait, landscape, zoom and orbit, keyboard interaction, reduced motion, hidden-tab resume, and rapid move/undo/retry. Check a physical phone before setting any performance claim. Use a 30 fps frame budget on the agreed reference phone as a provisional target and compare frame times against the existing build.
3. **Apply the accepted cues in batches.** Start with heavy objects and vehicles, then water/ice/beam feedback, then quiet placement and completion cues. Track the sprite download size and active effect count. Keep the hub free of game effects.
4. **Keep a rules regression gate.** Run the full suite and the production build after implementation. For each game, play one successful and one blocked or invalid interaction where applicable, verify undo/reload, and check that effects do not alter solver outcomes or saved progress. Treat any change to actual puzzle physics as a separate design decision.

## Implementation status

The three pilots and the rollout to all 21 games are implemented in the local build. The [pilot review](docs/FX_REVIEW.md) includes released and updated frames plus the effect strips. The proposed cues were adapted to each scene. The puzzle engines still decide outcomes.

The preview runs at http://127.0.0.1:4175/. All 454 tests and the production build pass. Browser checks covered the Petals mine and retry, the Water Balance pour and undo, both Rolling Stone landings, and first-level wins and reload for the other 18 games. Physical-phone frame times and a full device accessibility pass remain open.
